A co-op extraction game

Plunderspell

Four wizards. One castle. You cast by shouting the words out loud — and everything you steal, you have to physically carry home.

The pitch

It's a heist film, except everyone is an idiot with a wand

You and three friends are failing wizards. You share a damp lair, a stack of unpaid debts, and a portal that opens onto the past. Every night you step through it, break into somewhere you absolutely should not be, and try to get back out with something worth more than your own life.

Extraction games are built on one feeling: I have too much to lose and not enough time. Plunderspell keeps that and adds two things nobody has properly combined.

First: you cast spells with your actual voice. Not a hotkey. You press a key and say FRANGO and the wall breaks. Your friends hear you say it, half a second before it happens. When six people are whisper-shouting Latin at a locked door while something enormous walks toward them, you have a clip. Every single session produces a clip.

Second: loot is physical, not a grid. There is no inventory screen. A golden altarpiece is an object with mass that two people have to carry between them, and it will not fit through the window you came in by. You will drop things. You will throw a reliquary at a knight and watch it shatter and know exactly how much money just left the room.

The comedy isn't written into the game. It's what happens when physics and a microphone are both allowed to go wrong at once.
Why this, why now

The gap on the shelf

The proven appetite

Four-player co-op horror-comedy is the most reliably viral shape in PC gaming. Its titles sell on friends recording friends. It needs almost no marketing budget — it needs a mechanic that generates footage.

The unserved fantasy

That entire genre is set in space stations, sewers and abandoned facilities. Nobody has taken the formula somewhere with gold in it. Castles, crypts, treasuries and cathedrals are the most loot-dense fiction that exists, and the shelf is empty.

The unclaimed input

Everybody already has a microphone open, because these games run on voice chat. Using that mic as a control input costs the player nothing and has never been the core verb of a co-op game. It is sitting there, free.

The loop · 25–40 minutes

Lair, portal, castle, run

One raid is a closed loop. The lair is the only thing that persists, which is what makes losing a run hurt and makes a good one worth bragging about.

01 · PREPARE

The Lair

Persistent hub. Bind spells to your tongue, pick a physical weapon, choose an Age, and decide how greedy you're feeling. Debts are due whether you go out or not.

02 · COMMIT

The Portal

Step through into a procedurally built castle in a chosen century. Once the four of you are in, the portal narrows. It will not stay open all night.

03 · TAKE

The Plunder

Break in. Find the treasury, the crypt, the reliquary. Every object has weight, value and fragility. Noise attracts the household. So does shouting.

04 · PANIC

The Hue & Cry

Something notices. The castle wakes. Now you're carrying a chandelier down a spiral stair with a war-hound behind you and a spell you keep mispronouncing.

05 · ESCAPE

The Extraction

Only what physically crosses the portal comes home. Drop a friend's body or drop the gold — that is a genuine choice, and it will be remembered.

Pillar one · The tongue

Spells you have to say out loud

Hold the cast key. Speak the word. The game listens on your machine — no servers, no subscription, no audio ever leaves your PC — and recognises it in under a tenth of a second.

The lexicon is deliberately small and deliberately hard. Around forty words, chosen so they sound nothing like each other when whispered but tempting to slur when panicking. Mishearing is not a bug we're hiding — it is content we are writing. Every incantation has a documented failure twin, and the failure is always funnier than the success.

Runs offline

A small keyword-spotting model ships inside the build. No API key, no per-player cost, no outage can take the game down.

Accessible by default

Every incantation is also bindable to a key. Voice is the intended way to play, never the required one — nobody is locked out for being mute, shy, or in a shared house.

Volume is a mechanic

Loudness is read as well as words. Some spells demand a whisper; some demand a genuine shout. The game asks you to be brave in your own living room.

Pillar two · The hands

Nothing in the world is a menu

Every item is a rigid body with mass, and every rigid body can hurt someone. There is no separate "combat system" — hitting a man with a candlestick and looting a candlestick are the same code path.

Damage comes from impact velocity, so a sword is simply a heavy thing with a sharp end that you swung fast. That one rule makes the entire castle into a weapon: a dropped portcullis, a shoved wardrobe, a chandelier cut loose above a dining hall, a friend flung by a mispronounced TONITRUS.

It also makes greed legible without a single UI element. Bulk is measured in stone. A silver ewer is 2 stone and fits under one arm. The altarpiece is 14 stone, needs two people, and the moment either of you lets go it falls down the stairs and stops being worth anything.

Carry & hurl

Grab anything, rotate it in front of you, push it deeper or pull it closer, then throw. Already implemented on the existing codebase — including creatures, who can be picked up, carried, throttled and thrown.

Fragility

Stained glass, reliquaries, illuminated manuscripts and alchemical glass all break on impact. The most valuable loot in the game is also the loot least able to survive you panicking.

Corpses are objects

A dead friend is a 12-stone item. You can carry them out and revive them at the lair — at the cost of the gold you'd otherwise have carried in that pair of hands.

The castle reacts

Noise is a physical event, not a scripted trigger. A shattered window, a shouted incantation, a dropped suit of armour — each propagates as sound through the level and wakes what it reaches.

Pillar three · The Ages

Dig down through history and steal from all of it

Your lair sits outside time, so every Age is a destination rather than a chapter. Each has its own architecture, loot, defenders and weapon tier. The rule that makes this sing: whatever you carry out of one Age, you can carry into another. A wheellock pistol is a curiosity in 1620 and an act of god in 1200 BC. We will not balance that away — that is the progression.

Level generation

Castles aren't dungeons, so don't generate them like one

Every procedural co-op game builds a web of rooms and corridors. A real castle isn't that — it's a set of nested rings, each harder to get into than the last, and that structure does the game design for us for free.

We generate outward-in: curtain wall → outer bailey → inner ward → keep → undercroft and crypt. Value rises with every ring you cross. Which means the deepest, richest room is also the furthest from the way out, and extraction is always a fighting retreat back through everything you already woke up. You never walk out of a good run calmly.

Socket-matched modules

Rooms are prefabs with tagged joins — door, window, arrow-loop, stair up, stair down, murder-hole. Assembly matches sockets, so results stay architecturally plausible and artists can add rooms without a programmer.

One integer on the wire

The host rolls a seed and sends it before load. Every client builds the identical castle locally. No geometry is ever replicated, so a 400-room fortress costs the same bandwidth as an empty room.

Validated, not hoped-for

After generation, a flood-fill proves the crypt can reach the portal. If it can't, the castle is thrown away and rerolled. A layout that soft-locks a run is worse than a slow loading screen.

Art direction · Colour

Ground your own pigments

The palette is named for the pigments a real illuminator would have ground in the period the game is set. That isn't decoration — it constrains us honestly, because these are the only colours that could exist in the world we're depicting.

The discipline: verdigris carries the interface, orpiment is reserved exclusively for things worth money, and nothing else in the game is allowed to be gold. When a player sees that yellow, it always means the same thing.

Art direction · Mood

Six frames the game should be able to produce

Stylised, not photoreal — chunky readable silhouettes, hand-painted texture, and lighting that is almost entirely diegetic. If you can see it, something in the room is burning.

The Lair
One candle, one fire, deep warm falloff into total black. The only safe frame in the game.
The Treasury
Darkness with gold bleeding in from one corner. Orpiment doing its single job.
The Portal
Cold lapis light thrown upward onto faces. The only unnatural colour in the world.
The Hue & Cry
Madder and flame from below. Everything readable as silhouette against fire.
The Approach
Pre-dawn verdigris mist. Wet stone, low contrast, the long walk in.
Bronze Age Interior
Ochre plaster and smoke. Warmer, lower, dustier than every later Age.
Silhouette first

Robes, hoods and bulky loot read at 10 metres in the dark. Every character reads as a shape before it reads as a person.

Diegetic light only

Torches, candles, hearths, spell effects. No ambient fill. Darkness is a real obstacle, and light is a resource you carry and can drop.

Texture over polygons

Hand-painted albedo, soot in the crevices, gilt worn off the high points. Cheap to author, ages well, doesn't chase fidelity we can't win.

Production reality

Most of this is already built

This is the part that should change how seriously you take the pitch. Your existing repository — specifically the steam-multiplayer-framework branch, which is 30 commits ahead of everything else — is not a blank Unity project. It already contains a working first-person physics extraction game with Steam multiplayer. And it already has a SpellBook.

The single largest piece of work in pivoting is deletion. Planetary gravity lives in its own assembly, Runtime/Gravity/, and is exactly two files with three consumers. Removing it is an afternoon, not a rewrite.

The route in

Four milestones to knowing whether it's fun

The riskiest assumption is not the castle or the loot. It's whether shouting at your computer feels powerful rather than embarrassing. Everything is sequenced to answer that as early as possible.

Milestone 0
~1 week

Fork clean, cut gravity

Branch from the real trunk, delete the Gravity assembly, restore world gravity across the player states and items, confirm nothing else regressed.

  • Crates and players fall along −Y and land flat
  • Thrown items still deal velocity-scaled damage
  • All seven assemblies compile with zero gravity references
Milestone 1
~2 weeks

Prove the voice

A bare grey room and four incantations. Nothing else. Measure recognition accuracy, latency and — most importantly — whether people keep doing it after ten minutes.

  • >90% top-1 across four accents on a 40-word lexicon
  • Under 150 ms from word-end to effect
  • Misfires land as jokes, not as frustration
Milestone 2
~6 weeks

The vertical slice

One castle, one Age (High Medieval), four spells, four players, the full loop end to end. This is the thing you show people.

  • Castle generator built fresh — concentric wards, socket-matched modules, one replicated seed
  • Loot with value, bulk and fragility; two-person carries
  • An extraction portal that only counts what physically crosses it
  • A lair that remembers what came back
Milestone 3
ongoing

Open the other Ages

Ages are content, not engineering, once the ScriptableObject exists. Each adds a room set, a loot table, an enemy roster and a weapon tier — and immediately multiplies every Age before it, because the weapons travel.

  • Bronze Age, Late Medieval, Age of Powder
  • The anachronism rule stays unbalanced on purpose
  • Lair progression, debts, and reasons to go back out
In one line

Four friends whisper-shouting dead languages at a locked door while carrying a stolen altarpiece down a staircase, badly.

That sentence is the entire marketing plan. It is also a description of the mechanics, which is how you know the design is sound.
